3D Printed Numpad with Teensy 4.0 running QMK

HardwareDIYMaker

I have always wanted to make a standalone number pad that compliments my primary NK87 mechanical keyboard. They are especially helpful when performing any kind of numeric data entry and ostensibly in showcasing those fancy custom keycaps that do not seem to fit with the main keycap theme (but still must be seen). This project helped me learn about the keyboard diode/wiring matrix as well as the underlying polling algorithm that the MCU runs, provided by the Quantum Mechanical Keyboard firmware - or QMK.

A luxury of having access to a 3D printer and surfing the world wide web is being able to find freely available models of almost any object you can think of and materializing it in no time. By no means am I an expert with 3D design and would have been hampered by the learning curve in modeling a num-pad from scratch in an application I'm not well versed in. So I gladly searched sites like thingiverse and printables for a model that fit my needs. I was looking for a model with ample space to fit the MCU and wiring. Something with a similarly sized MCU and a comfortable amount of room to mess up and make changes if needed.

Bottom plateTop plate

I gravitated towards Jebidiah_Crumps' numpad model because it was everything I was looking for. It allowed mechanical switches to be seated directly into the top shell. The designer modeled the number pad for the Arduino Pro Micro, which is close enough in size to the Teensy 4.0 that it could be made to mount as intended. The removable bottom plate allows me to easily solder & hardwire the matrix and have direct access to the MCU. The model has a slot for a USB connection to passthrough to the MCU. There is also a hole for a pushbutton that can be recessed into the wall of the shell. I definitely preferred this layout of the switches over others. I find it useful that there are 4 switches, a half-row above the rest, that could be repurposed to map user-defined functions, macros, or for QMK mode switching.

I thought it would be helpful for me to use KiCad schematic editor to drag & drop the components in order to better conceptualize how they connect. This is something I try to spend more time with, I think there is tremendous utility in being able to design, simulate, and prototype circuits from scratch. In the future, I'd like to try redesigning this number pad with a PCB that uses surface mount components and possibly an upgraded MCU allowing for the full QMK experience.

Going into this, I understood that the Teensy 4.0 was not the best choice as a MCU for running the QMK firmware. Because of a lack of ChibiOS support for this controller, features like EEPROM persistence and addressable LED lighting wouldn't be possible through QMK. The Teensy I have was gifted to me and was collecting dust in a bin of not-yet-undertaken projects. Feeling bad enough I hadn't gotten to it yet, I had to simply put it to use. Even though the Teensy4.0's microcontroller isn't officifally supported by QMK, I was undeterred. I found that Teensy 4.X controllers were actually being used in part of Michael Stapelberg's kinT project to replace controllers in the ergonomic Kinesis Advantage keyboards.

With ample documentation from QMK & kinx-project/kint, I was able to discern what needed to happen to flash the Teensy4.0 with QMK.

I want to give a huge shout-out and more attention to Michael Stapelberg and his work in getting the ball rolling for this line of chips. Using his work as a guide, I was able to modify the kint41 configuration and layout to suit my own numpad project. His contributions ultimately enabled me to have a fully-fledged keyboard firmware running on the Teensy 4.0. Surprisingly, there was minimal code for me to actually write - and I was okay with this. So, thank you so much!

I exalt the open-source / community-led projects and the contributors behind them for their willingness to share their passions with the world and empower others to do awesome things with their technology. It's because of people like Michael Stapelberg and Jebidiah_Crump, I'm inspired to create and contribute to open-source initiatives where possible without fear of ridicule. Their work and willingness to share information with the world reminds me that not every great idea has to be locked behind a subscription or trademark. I would not have been able to do this without things like kinx-project, KiCad, and the QMK firmware (and it's suite of tools).
